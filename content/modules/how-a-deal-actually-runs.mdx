A deal is a process with a shape. Knowing the shape tells you what is happening when a
headline says "talks are at an advanced stage", and where deals actually die.

## Two ways a company gets sold

**A broad auction.** The seller's bankers approach many potential buyers. Maximises
price through competition, at the cost of confidentiality — dozens of parties, including
competitors, learn the business is for sale.

**A targeted process or bilateral negotiation.** One or a handful of buyers.
Confidential and fast, with no competitive tension to push the price up.

The choice is a trade between price and secrecy, and it is one of the first pieces of
advice a banker gives.

## The sequence

**1. Preparation.** The seller's bankers build the materials: a short anonymous teaser,
then a detailed information memorandum for parties who sign a non-disclosure agreement.
A financial model is built and the business is tidied for inspection.

**2. First-round outreach.** Teasers go out. Interested parties sign NDAs, receive the
information memorandum, and submit **non-binding indications of interest** — a price
range, subject to everything.

**3. Second round.** A shortlist gets access to a **virtual data room**: contracts,
financials, litigation, employee data, intellectual property. They conduct due
diligence, meet management, and submit binding offers with markups of the seller's draft
purchase agreement.

**4. Negotiation and exclusivity.** The seller picks a winner, often granting
exclusivity for a period. Leverage shifts to the buyer the moment competition ends,
which is why sellers grant it late and briefly.

**5. Signing.** The purchase agreement is executed. The deal is agreed but not done.

**6. Between signing and closing.** Regulatory approvals, antitrust clearance,
shareholder votes, third-party consents. This can take months.

**7. Closing.** Money moves, ownership transfers.

<Callout type="note" title="Signing is not closing">
Press coverage treats the announcement as the end. It is the middle. Between signing and
closing sit the conditions, and the gap is where regulators intervene, financing markets
turn, and buyers develop second thoughts. The purchase agreement is largely a document
about what happens during that gap.
</Callout>

## Due diligence

Verification of what the seller has claimed. Financial diligence tests the numbers,
particularly the quality of earnings — are the profits recurring or flattered by one-off
items. Legal diligence examines contracts, litigation and ownership of key assets.
Commercial diligence asks whether the market is really as described. Tax, IT, HR and
environmental follow.

Diligence rarely kills a deal outright. Far more often it moves the price, or converts a
discovered problem into a specific indemnity in the agreement.

## What the agreement actually does

Three jobs, all about allocating risk:

- **Representations and warranties.** Statements of fact by the seller, with remedies if
  untrue.
- **Conditions precedent.** What must happen before closing.
- **Termination provisions and break fees.** Who may walk away, when, and at what cost.
  A reverse break fee — payable by the buyer if it fails to obtain antitrust clearance —
  is often the single most negotiated number in the document.

## Where deals die

- Price gaps that diligence widens rather than closes
- Financing that disappears when credit markets turn
- Regulatory objection, especially where the parties compete directly
- Leaks that bring in a rival bidder or spook customers and staff
- Simple loss of nerve on either side

## What to take away

1. Auction versus targeted process is a trade between price and confidentiality.
2. Non-binding indications, then diligence, then binding offers. Exclusivity comes late.
3. Signing is not closing; the gap between them is what the agreement governs.
4. Diligence usually moves price or creates indemnities rather than ending a deal.
