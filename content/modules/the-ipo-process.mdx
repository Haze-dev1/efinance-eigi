An initial public offering is a company selling shares to the public for the first time.
It takes months, costs a great deal, and permanently changes how the company operates.

## Why go public at all

**Raise capital** at a scale private markets may not provide, repeatedly, once listed.

**Give existing shareholders an exit.** Founders, employees and early investors hold
shares they cannot easily sell. A listing creates a market for them.

**Currency and credibility.** Listed shares can be used to make acquisitions and to pay
staff. A listing also brings a level of scrutiny that some customers and counterparties
treat as reassurance.

Against that: continuous disclosure, quarterly earnings pressure, a share price that
reacts to news outside management's control, and substantial ongoing compliance cost.
Plenty of companies that could list choose not to.

## The sequence

**1. Appoint banks.** The lead manager — sometimes called the bookrunner — runs the
process. Large deals have several.

**2. Due diligence and drafting.** The offer document is prepared: business description,
risk factors, audited financials, use of proceeds, related-party transactions. This is
the heaviest single piece of work.

**3. File with the regulator.** In India the draft red herring prospectus goes to SEBI;
in the US the registration statement goes to the SEC. The regulator comments; the
document is revised, sometimes over several rounds.

**4. Marketing.** Research, then a roadshow of management meetings with institutional
investors.

**5. Book-building.** A price range is set, and investors submit bids at prices within
it. The bookrunner builds a picture of demand at each level — this is the **book**.

**6. Pricing and allocation.** A final price is set from the book. Shares are allocated,
usually with a deliberate bias toward investors expected to hold rather than sell
immediately.

**7. Listing.** Trading begins. Everything from here is secondary market.

## The first-day pop

Shares frequently close their first day well above the offer price. This is usually
reported as a triumph. Consider who paid for it.

If a company sells 10 crore shares at ₹100 and they close at ₹140, the company raised
₹1,000 crore for shares the market immediately valued at ₹1,400 crore. That ₹400 crore
went to the investors who received an allocation, not to the company. It is often called
**money left on the table**, and it is a real transfer.

<Callout type="warning" title="Why underpricing persists">
There are honest explanations. Some underpricing compensates investors for committing
before there is any trading history. Nobody can price a first issue exactly, and a
failed offering is far more damaging than a cheap one.

But note the incentives. The bank's fee is a percentage of the amount raised, so
underpricing costs it a little. The same bank allocates the underpriced shares to
institutional clients it deals with repeatedly. A pop makes those clients happy at the
issuer's expense.

None of this makes a first-day rise sinister. It does mean it is not straightforwardly
good news for the company, and the framing in most coverage gets that backwards.
</Callout>

## Lock-ups and stabilisation

**Lock-up agreements** stop insiders selling for a period after listing, commonly six
months. Without them the market would reasonably assume anyone selling knows something.
Lock-up expiry is a scheduled, well-known event and often moves the price.

The **greenshoe**, or over-allotment option, lets the bookrunner sell more shares than
planned and then buy in the market if the price falls, supporting it. It is a
stabilisation mechanism with defined limits and a defined period, disclosed in the offer
document.

## Alternatives

**Direct listing** — existing shares are listed without issuing new ones. No capital
raised, no underwriting, no lock-up, and no first-day money left on the table.

**Follow-on offering** — a further issue by an already-listed company. Much simpler,
because the market already prices the shares.

## What to take away

1. A listing buys capital access and liquidity at the cost of disclosure and scrutiny.
2. Regulatory review is of disclosure, and it happens before pricing.
3. Book-building discovers demand; the price comes from the book, not from a formula.
4. A large first-day pop is a transfer from the company to allocated investors.
