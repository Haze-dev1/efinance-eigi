Ask three bankers to value the same company and you will get three numbers. This is
often taken as evidence that valuation is arbitrary. It is closer to the opposite: the
numbers differ because they are answering different questions.

## Value is not price

**Price** is what something trades at. It is observable, it changes by the second, and
it is the outcome of everyone's opinions colliding.

**Value** is an estimate of what something is worth on a set of assumptions. It is not
observable. Two careful analysts can disagree without either being careless.

Most of investing is a bet that these two have come apart and will converge again.

## The three methods

**Intrinsic valuation — discounted cash flow.** Forecast the cash the business will
generate, discount it back to today at a rate reflecting its risk, add it up. This is
the only method that asks what the business is actually worth rather than what someone
else would pay. It is also the most sensitive to assumptions, and a DCF can be pushed
to almost any answer by someone who wants one.

**Relative valuation — comparable companies.** Find similar listed businesses, see what
multiple of earnings or revenue they trade at, apply it. Fast, grounded in real market
prices, and it inherits whatever mispricing the market currently has. In a bubble,
comps say everything is worth a bubble price.

**Transaction valuation — precedent transactions.** Look at what buyers actually paid
for similar companies. Because acquirers pay a premium for control, this typically
gives the highest number of the three.

<Callout type="note" title="Why the football field exists">
Bankers present valuation as a bar chart of ranges — one bar per method — called a
football field. It is not indecision. Each method answers a distinct question: what the
cash is worth, what the market pays for similar businesses today, and what a buyer paid
for control. A single point estimate would hide the disagreement that is the most
informative part.
</Callout>

## Equity value and enterprise value

The single most common beginner error is comparing the wrong two numbers.

**Equity value** — often called market capitalisation for a listed company — is share
price times shares outstanding. It is the value of the shareholders' claim.

**Enterprise value** is the value of the whole operating business, regardless of who
financed it:

    Enterprise value = equity value + debt − cash

Two identical businesses with identical operations can have very different equity values
purely because one borrowed and one did not. Enterprise value strips that out, which is
why it is the right basis for comparison.

## A worked intuition

Two shops earn the same ₹10 crore a year.

- Shop A: no debt, ₹5 crore cash in the bank. Equity value ₹100 crore.
- Shop B: ₹40 crore debt, no cash. Equity value ₹60 crore.

Shop B looks half the price. It is not. Enterprise values are ₹95 crore and ₹100 crore
respectively — nearly identical, which is what you would expect from identical
operations. The equity gap is entirely a financing difference.

## Where valuation actually goes wrong

Rarely in the arithmetic. Usually in one of these:

- **Anchoring on a number the client wants**, then working backwards to assumptions
  that produce it.
- **Comparables that are not comparable.** Same industry code, different growth,
  margins, or geography.
- **Growth assumptions that never fade.** Nothing compounds above the economy forever.
- **Ignoring the balance sheet**, i.e. the equity-versus-enterprise error above.

## What to take away

1. Price is observable; value is estimated. They come apart, which is the opportunity.
2. Three methods, three questions: intrinsic worth, current market pricing, price paid
   for control.
3. Enterprise value compares businesses; equity value compares shareholder claims.
4. Valuations fail on assumptions and comparability, almost never on arithmetic.
