A credit rating is one agency's opinion about how likely a borrower is to default. It is
not a measurement, not a guarantee, and not an opinion about whether the security is
worth its price. It is also enormously consequential, which is the tension.

## The scales

Three agencies dominate globally: S&P, Moody's and Fitch. India has its own recognised
agencies including CRISIL, ICRA and CARE.

The scales differ in notation but not in structure. Taking S&P's:

**Investment grade** — AAA, AA, A, BBB
**Speculative grade** — BB, B, CCC, CC, C, D

Ratings carry modifiers (+/− or 1/2/3) within each band.

## The line that matters

The boundary between BBB− and BB+ is not one step among many. It is a cliff, because it
is written into other people's rules.

Many institutional investors — pension funds, insurers, some mutual funds — are
restricted by mandate or regulation to investment grade. When an issuer is downgraded
across that line, those holders are not deciding whether to sell. They are required to.

The result is forced selling into a falling market by holders who have no discretion.
This is why a single notch of downgrade can move a bond's price far more than the
change in default probability would justify, and why issuers manage their balance sheets
around defending the boundary.

<Callout type="warning" title="The issuer pays for the rating">
Under the dominant model, the company being rated pays the agency that rates it.

The conflict is obvious: the agency's customer is the entity whose creditworthiness it
is assessing, and that customer can take its business elsewhere. Agencies point to
reputational incentives, internal separation of analysts from commercial staff, and
regulatory oversight — in the US, the SEC's Office of Credit Ratings supervises
registered agencies.

Those mitigations exist because the conflict is real. In the run-up to 2008, large
volumes of structured mortgage securities carried AAA ratings and subsequently defaulted
in numbers no AAA rating implied. This is documented history, not speculation, and it is
the single best argument for treating a rating as an input rather than an answer.
</Callout>

## What a rating does and does not say

**Does:** express a relative opinion on the likelihood of default, and in some cases on
expected recovery if default occurs.

**Does not:** say the price is fair. A CCC bond can be an excellent investment at a low
enough price, and a AAA bond a poor one at a high enough price. Ratings assess credit
risk, not value.

**Does not:** predict timing, or account for market or liquidity risk.

## Why issuers care so much

- **Cost.** Each notch translates into basis points on every rupee borrowed, over years.
- **Access.** Some investors cannot buy below a threshold at any price.
- **Covenants.** Loan terms sometimes reference ratings, with rate step-ups on
  downgrade — which raises the cost precisely when the company can least afford it.
- **Trading relationships.** Counterparties may demand more collateral from a
  lower-rated firm.

## What actually drives a rating

Roughly two halves.

**Business risk:** industry cyclicality, competitive position, scale, diversification,
country risk.

**Financial risk:** leverage, interest coverage, cash flow relative to debt, liquidity
and maturity profile, and financial policy — how the management team has behaved
historically toward debt.

That last one carries real weight. An agency assesses stated intentions against a track
record.

## What to take away

1. A rating is an opinion on default risk, not on value or price.
2. The investment-grade boundary is a cliff because other people's mandates make it one.
3. The issuer-pays model is a real conflict with a documented failure history.
4. Ratings are an input to your own analysis, never a replacement for it.
