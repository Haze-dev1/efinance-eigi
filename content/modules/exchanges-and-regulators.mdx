Two institutions stand between a saver and a company: an exchange and a regulator. They
are routinely confused, including by people who own shares. They do entirely different
jobs.

## What an exchange actually does

An exchange is a matching engine with rules attached. Its job is to let a buyer and a
seller who have never met transact safely and at a fair price. Concretely it provides:

**Price discovery.** Continuous public quoting means the price reflects what people
will actually pay right now, not what someone asserts the business is worth.

**Liquidity.** Enough participants in one place that you can sell without moving the
price much. Liquidity is not a fixed property — it evaporates exactly when everyone
wants it.

**Settlement and counterparty risk removal.** This is the invisible one and arguably the
most important. A clearing corporation steps in between the two sides so that if your
counterparty fails, you are still paid. Without it, every trade would require you to
assess a stranger's creditworthiness.

**Listing standards.** Minimum disclosure, governance and free-float requirements for a
company to be quoted at all.

## What a regulator does

A securities regulator — SEBI in India, the SEC in the United States — writes and
enforces the rules of that market. Registration of intermediaries, disclosure
requirements, insider trading enforcement, market conduct.

<Callout type="warning" title="Disclosure review is not merit review">
Neither SEBI nor the SEC tells you whether an investment is good. They review whether
the risks were disclosed adequately and in the prescribed form. A cleared offer document
means the company said enough, in the right way, about how you might lose money. It is
not an opinion that you will not.

Plenty of duly-cleared IPOs have gone on to lose investors most of their money, and
nothing about that is a regulatory failure. That was always the deal.
</Callout>

## Primary and secondary regulation

The regulator's grip is tightest at the moment of issue. A company selling securities to
the public files a detailed document, the regulator comments, the document is revised.
That is a heavyweight process, and it exists because that is the point where the
information gap between company and investor is widest.

Once trading, oversight shifts to ongoing obligations: periodic financial reporting,
prompt disclosure of material events, restrictions on trading by insiders. The theory
is that a market can price a security correctly only if everyone is looking at the same
facts at the same time.

## Who else is in the chain

- **Clearing corporations** guarantee settlement.
- **Depositories** hold securities electronically, so shares are book entries rather
  than paper certificates.
- **Registrars and transfer agents** maintain the ownership record.
- **Custodians** hold assets on behalf of large institutions.

None of these are glamorous, and all of them are load-bearing. Most of what people call
"the market working" is really this plumbing not failing.

## Exchanges are companies too

Worth noticing: major exchanges are themselves listed, for-profit companies. They earn
transaction fees, listing fees, and increasingly the largest share from selling market
data. An institution that both writes listing standards and profits from listings has an
obvious tension in it, which is part of why a separate statutory regulator exists.

## What to take away

1. An exchange provides price discovery, liquidity, settlement and listing standards.
2. A regulator writes and enforces rules; it does not vouch for quality.
3. Disclosure review means "you were told", never "this is sound".
4. The settlement plumbing is the part you only notice when it breaks.
