A discounted cash flow model rests on one idea: a business is worth the cash it will
produce, adjusted for the fact that cash arriving later is worth less than cash
arriving now.

Everything difficult is hidden inside "will produce".

## Why later cash is worth less

₹100 today beats ₹100 in three years, for three separate reasons: you could invest it
in the meantime, inflation erodes it, and the future payment might not arrive.

The **discount rate** bundles all three. To find what a future amount is worth today:

    Present value = Future cash / (1 + r)^n

At a 10% discount rate, ₹100 in five years is worth about ₹62 today. At 15% it is about
₹50. The rate matters enormously, which is why the next chapter is entirely about it.

## Free cash flow

A DCF discounts **unlevered free cash flow** — cash generated by the operating business
before any payments to lenders. Because it is pre-financing, it belongs to all
capital providers, so discounting it gives you **enterprise value**.

The build:

    EBIT
    − taxes on EBIT
    = net operating profit after tax
    + depreciation and amortisation      (non-cash, already deducted)
    − capital expenditure                (real cash out, not in EBIT)
    − increase in working capital        (growth ties up cash)
    = unlevered free cash flow

The last two lines are where optimistic models cheat. Growth requires capital
expenditure and working capital. A forecast showing revenue doubling with capex flat is
not a forecast, it is a wish.

## The forecast horizon

Typically five to ten years. Not because anyone can see ten years ahead, but because the
model has to stop somewhere and hand over to a terminal value.

This is the honest tension in a DCF: the years you can forecast with any confidence
contribute a minority of the value, and the years you cannot contribute the majority.
For a growing business, the terminal value is routinely 60–80% of the total.

<Callout type="warning" title="Where DCFs are actually manipulated">
Not in the arithmetic. In three inputs:

**Revenue growth** — extended a couple of points too long.
**Terminal growth rate** — nudged from 2% to 3%, which can move the answer 20% or more.
**Discount rate** — shaved by half a point.

Each looks defensible alone. Together they can move a valuation by half. When you are
handed a DCF, go straight to those three cells.
</Callout>

## From enterprise value to a share price

Discounting unlevered cash flows gives enterprise value. To get to what a share is
worth, reverse the bridge:

    Enterprise value
    − debt
    + cash
    = equity value
    ÷ diluted shares outstanding
    = value per share

Forgetting this step and dividing enterprise value by share count is a common and
badly wrong mistake — it credits shareholders with value that belongs to lenders.

## What a DCF is actually good for

Its output is a number, but its value is the argument. Building one forces you to state
what you believe about growth, margins, reinvestment and risk, in a form where each
belief is visible and can be challenged.

A DCF that disagrees with the market price is not proof the market is wrong. It is a
prompt: which of my assumptions differs from the consensus, and do I have a reason?

## What to take away

1. A business is worth its future cash, discounted for time and risk.
2. Unlevered free cash flow is pre-financing, so it discounts to enterprise value.
3. Capex and working capital are where growth gets paid for; models that skip them lie.
4. Bridge from enterprise value to equity value before dividing by share count.
