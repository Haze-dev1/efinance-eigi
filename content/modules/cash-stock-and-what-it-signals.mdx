How a deal is paid for is usually reported as a detail. It is not. The choice between
cash and shares reallocates risk between the two sets of shareholders, and it leaks what
the buyer privately believes about its own share price.

## What each does

**Cash.** The target's shareholders receive a fixed amount and leave. Their outcome is
certain the moment the deal closes. The buyer's shareholders keep 100% of whatever
happens next — all the synergies if it works, all the damage if it does not.

**Shares.** The target's shareholders become the buyer's shareholders. They now own a
slice of the combined company, so they share in the synergies and in the failures. The
buyer's existing shareholders are diluted, but they have transferred part of the risk.

That is the trade in one sentence: **cash concentrates the outcome on the buyer's
shareholders; stock shares it.**

## The signalling problem

Management knows more about its own company than the market does. So the choice of
currency carries information.

If you genuinely believe your shares are undervalued, paying with them is expensive —
you are handing over something worth more than the market says. You would rather pay
cash.

If you believe your shares are overvalued, they are cheap currency. Paying with them is
attractive precisely because you are handing over something worth less than the market
thinks.

<Callout type="warning" title="What the market infers, and why announcements move prices">
Because investors understand this reasoning, a large all-stock acquisition is often read
as a signal that management thinks its own shares are expensive. Acquirer share prices
frequently fall on announcement of stock-funded deals, and that reaction is partly this
inference rather than a judgement on the target.

This is a tendency, not a law. Stock is also used simply because a deal is too large to
fund with cash, or because the buyer wants the seller to stay invested in the outcome.
</Callout>

## What else drives the choice

**Size.** Some deals are too big to pay for in cash at any sensible leverage.

**The buyer's balance sheet.** A company already carrying significant debt may not be
able to borrow more without a credit downgrade.

**Tax.** In many jurisdictions receiving cash triggers an immediate capital gains
charge for the seller, while a share-for-share exchange can defer it. A seller may
accept a lower headline price in shares than in cash for exactly this reason.

**Who the seller is.** A founder retiring wants cash. A founder who believes in the
combination and will stay on may prefer shares — and the buyer may want them to hold
shares, precisely so their incentives stay aligned after closing.

**Certainty.** Cash is certain. A stock deal's value moves with the buyer's share price
between signing and closing, which is why stock deals often include a **collar** — a
mechanism that adjusts the exchange ratio if the price moves beyond agreed bounds.

## Mixed consideration

Most large deals are a blend: some cash, some shares, sometimes with an **earn-out**
where part of the price depends on the target hitting agreed targets after closing.

Earn-outs are a way of bridging a disagreement about value. The buyer thinks the
forecast is optimistic; the seller insists it is achievable; the earn-out says *prove
it and you will be paid*. They are also a reliable source of litigation, because the
buyer controls the business whose performance determines what it must pay.

## What to take away

1. Cash concentrates risk and reward on the buyer's shareholders; stock shares both.
2. The currency choice signals what management believes about its own valuation.
3. Size, leverage capacity, tax treatment and seller preference all constrain the choice.
4. Earn-outs bridge valuation disagreements and reliably create disputes.
