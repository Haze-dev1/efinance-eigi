Every acquisition announcement contains the word *synergy*. It is doing a lot of work,
and most of it is concealment. Underneath are a small number of recurring motives, some
sound and some not.

## The sound reasons

**Cost synergies.** Two companies, one head office, one finance team, one set of
systems. These are the most reliable synergies because they are subtractions: you can
count the roles being removed. They are also the reason acquisitions produce
redundancies, which is why the word is avoided in the press release.

**Revenue synergies.** Sell your product through their distribution, or their product to
your customers. Genuine when it happens, and much less reliable than cost synergies
because it depends on customers behaving as hoped rather than on a spreadsheet
subtraction.

**Buying capability faster than building it.** A pharmaceutical company buys a biotech
for a drug in trials. A bank buys a fintech for its payments stack. The question is
always the same: is the price below what building it would cost, discounted for the time
saved and the risk avoided?

**Scale where scale genuinely matters.** In businesses with high fixed costs, spreading
those over more volume is real. In businesses without them, "scale" is a slogan.

**Consolidating a fragmented market.** Buying many small competitors to gain pricing
power and purchasing leverage.

## The unsound ones

**Empire building.** Chief executives of larger companies are paid more and are harder
to remove. This is a real and well-documented incentive, and it is not usually stated
out loud.

**Diversification for its own sake.** A conglomerate reduces its earnings volatility by
owning unrelated businesses. But shareholders can diversify themselves by buying both
companies' shares, far more cheaply and without paying a control premium. Diversifying
on the shareholders' behalf mostly destroys value — the phenomenon is well enough
documented to have a name, the conglomerate discount.

**Momentum.** Once a process has run for six months, with fees incurred and a board
briefed, stopping requires someone to say the last six months were wasted. Deals
acquire their own inertia.

<Callout type="warning" title="The control premium sets the bar">
An acquirer typically pays 20–40% above the target's market price. That premium is the
hurdle. Synergies must exceed it before the buyer's shareholders are any better off —
because without synergies, the buyer has simply paid more than market for something the
market was already pricing.

This is why the honest test is not "is the target a good business" but "is it worth
more to us than to anyone else, by more than the premium".
</Callout>

## Who captures the value

If a buyer pays a 30% premium and achieves synergies worth 30% of the target's value,
the target's shareholders have captured all of it. The buyer's shareholders are exactly
where they started, having taken on integration risk for nothing.

This is roughly what the evidence suggests happens on average: target shareholders do
well, acquirer shareholders do not. That average conceals wide variation, and the
variation is the interesting part — it tends to track how disciplined the buyer was on
price and how genuine the cost synergies were.

## What to take away

1. Cost synergies are countable and reliable; revenue synergies are neither.
2. The control premium is the hurdle synergies must clear before value is created.
3. Diversification on shareholders' behalf usually destroys value — they can do it
   themselves for free.
4. On average target shareholders capture the gains. Price discipline is what separates
   the exceptions.
