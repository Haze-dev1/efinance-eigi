A private equity firm buys a company for ₹1,000 crore. It puts in ₹300 crore of its own
money and borrows the other ₹700 crore — against the company being bought, not against
the fund. That structure is a leveraged buyout, and the mechanism deserves to be
understood precisely, because it is widely described either as financial alchemy or as
looting, and it is neither.

## The arithmetic

Buy at ₹1,000 crore, sell five years later at ₹1,500 crore.

**Unleveraged.** ₹1,000 crore in, ₹1,500 crore out. You made 1.5x your money.

**Leveraged.** ₹300 crore equity, ₹700 crore debt. Over five years the company's own
cash flow repays ₹300 crore of that debt, leaving ₹400 crore outstanding at exit. You
sell for ₹1,500 crore, repay ₹400 crore, and keep ₹1,100 crore. Against ₹300 crore
invested, that is **3.7x**.

Same company, same sale price, same five years. The return more than doubled.

<Callout type="warning" title="Leverage does not create value. It concentrates it.">
The business performed identically in both cases. What changed is how the gain was
split. With less equity in the deal, each rupee of gain is spread over fewer rupees of
your money.

The symmetry is exact and it is the part that gets forgotten. Sell at ₹800 crore
instead. Unleveraged you lose 20%. Leveraged, after repaying ₹400 crore of debt, you
receive ₹400 crore on ₹300 crore invested — still a gain. But sell at ₹500 crore:
unleveraged you lose half your money; leveraged you receive ₹100 crore on ₹300 crore
and have lost two thirds. Push a little further and the equity is worth nothing at all
while a lender still recovers most of its money.

## Where the returns actually come from

Three sources, and only one is reliable.

**Deleveraging.** The company's cash flow repays debt. Every rupee repaid converts a
lender's claim into the sponsor's equity. This works as long as the business generates
cash, and it is the workhorse of most buyout returns.

**Operational improvement.** Growing revenue, widening margins, exiting unprofitable
lines. Real value creation, and the only one of the three where the business is
genuinely better off.

**Multiple expansion.** Buying at 8x EBITDA and selling at 10x. Pleasant, but it depends
on the market being more generous at exit than at entry — which is not something a buyer
can underwrite. Sponsors who *rely* on it are making a bet on market conditions and
calling it a strategy.

## What makes a company a good LBO candidate

The requirements follow directly from the fact that the debt must be serviced:

- **Predictable cash flow.** Ideally contracted or subscription-based. A cyclical
  business can have a bad year at exactly the wrong moment.
- **Low existing debt**, so there is capacity to add more.
- **Modest capital expenditure needs**, so cash is available for debt service rather
  than being consumed by the business.
- **A defensible market position**, so margins survive the holding period.
- **Assets that can be pledged**, which lowers the borrowing cost.
- **A credible exit**: a trade buyer, another sponsor, or a public listing.

Which is why buyouts cluster in software with recurring revenue, business services,
healthcare and consumer staples — and are rare in early-stage biotech or heavy
commodities.

## What the debt does to behaviour

There is a real argument, made seriously since the 1980s, that debt improves
management. Cash committed to interest cannot be spent on empire building, and a
quarterly obligation focuses attention. Some of the operational discipline in buyouts
is genuinely produced by the leverage.

The same mechanism removes the margin for error. A firm with no debt survives two bad
years. The same firm at six times EBITDA may not survive one.

## What to take away

1. Leverage concentrates returns; it does not create them. The downside concentrates
   identically.
2. Deleveraging and operational improvement are underwritable; multiple expansion is a
   market bet.
3. Good candidates have predictable cash flow, low existing debt and modest capex.
4. Debt imposes discipline and removes the margin for error, and those are the same fact.
