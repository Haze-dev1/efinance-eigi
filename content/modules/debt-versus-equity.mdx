A company needs ₹500 crore. It can borrow it or it can sell part of itself. Almost
every question in corporate finance eventually reduces to some version of that choice,
so it is worth being precise about what actually differs.

## The two instruments

**Debt** is a promise. The company owes a fixed amount on fixed dates. Lenders do not
own the business, do not vote, and do not share in the upside. If the company triples
in value, the lender still gets exactly the interest and principal it was promised.

**Equity** is ownership. Shareholders have no promise of anything. They get what is
left after everyone else is paid — which may be enormous, or may be nothing.

## The waterfall is the whole idea

When a company is wound up, claims are paid in a strict order:

1. Secured lenders, against the specific assets pledged to them
2. Unsecured lenders and bondholders
3. Subordinated debt
4. Preference shares
5. Ordinary shareholders

Each level is paid in full before the next receives a rupee. Equity is last, which is
why it is called the **residual claim**.

<Callout type="note" title="Why the ordering explains the pricing">
Lenders accept a capped return because they are near the front of the queue.
Shareholders demand a higher expected return because they are at the back. Risk and
required return are the same fact seen from two ends — not two separate observations
that happen to correlate.
</Callout>

## What each costs

Debt looks cheaper, and partly is. The lender's return is capped and their claim is
senior, so they accept less. In most jurisdictions interest is also deductible against
taxable profit, so the government effectively subsidises part of the cost — the **tax
shield**.

But debt carries an obligation that does not care about circumstances. A coupon is due
on the date whether or not the quarter went well. Equity has no such date. A company
that skips a dividend disappoints its shareholders; a company that skips a coupon is in
default, and control of the outcome moves to its creditors.

So the honest comparison is not "debt is cheaper." It is: debt is cheaper *and* it
converts a variable cost into a fixed one, which raises the chance of ruin.

## Where the middle ground lives

The line is not clean, and the instruments in between are where much of banking
happens: convertible bonds that start as debt and become equity on a trigger,
preference shares that pay a fixed dividend but sit below debt, and mezzanine debt that
prices like equity because it is nearly as exposed.

## Capital structure

The mix of debt and equity a company uses is its **capital structure**, and there is a
long-running argument about whether the mix changes what the company is worth.

The starting point in the academic literature is that, under strong simplifying
assumptions — no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no information asymmetry — it does not.
Those assumptions are all false in practice, which is why the theory is a baseline
rather than an answer. Taxes push toward debt. Bankruptcy costs push away from it. Most
firms end up somewhere in between, and the somewhere moves with the interest rate
cycle.

## What to take away

1. Debt is a promise; equity is a residual claim. Everything else follows.
2. The liquidation waterfall explains why required returns differ by instrument.
3. Debt is cheaper partly for real reasons (seniority, tax shield) and partly because
   it moves risk rather than removing it.
4. A fixed obligation in a variable business is the mechanism by which companies fail.
