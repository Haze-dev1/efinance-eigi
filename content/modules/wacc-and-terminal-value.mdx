Two inputs decide most of a DCF's answer: the rate you discount at, and the number you
put on everything beyond the forecast. Both are estimates presented as precision.

## WACC: the blended cost of capital

A business is funded by lenders and shareholders, who require different returns. The
**weighted average cost of capital** blends them in proportion to how much each provides:

    WACC = (E/V × Re) + (D/V × Rd × (1 − t))

where E is equity value, D is debt, V is E + D, Re is the cost of equity, Rd the cost of
debt and t the tax rate.

Two things to notice. Debt is multiplied by (1 − t), because interest is generally
deductible and the government therefore bears part of the cost. And the weights are
market values, not book values — what the capital is worth now, not what it was raised
at.

## Cost of debt

The easy half. It is roughly what the company pays to borrow today — the yield on its
traded bonds, or the rate on recent loans. Not the historical average coupon: you want
the current cost of raising a new rupee.

## Cost of equity

The hard half, because shareholders have no contractual rate. The standard approach is
the capital asset pricing model:

    Re = risk-free rate + β × equity risk premium

- **Risk-free rate.** The yield on a long-dated government bond in the same currency.
- **Beta.** How much the stock moves relative to the market. Beta of 1.3 means it moves
  about 30% more than the index, in both directions.
- **Equity risk premium.** The extra return investors demand for holding equities rather
  than government bonds. Not observable — it is estimated from history or implied from
  current prices, and reasonable people put it anywhere from 4% to 7%.

<Callout type="note" title="CAPM's honest status">
CAPM is a convention, not a law. Its assumptions are strong and beta is unstable —
measure it over three years or five and you get different answers. It survives because
it is transparent and everyone uses it, so disagreements happen in the inputs rather
than in the framework. Treat its output as a defensible starting point, not a
measurement.
</Callout>

## Terminal value

The forecast stops; the business does not. Terminal value is everything after the final
forecast year, and it is usually the majority of the answer.

**Perpetuity growth (Gordon growth).** Assume cash flows grow at a constant rate forever:

    TV = FCF(final year) × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)

The constraint that matters: **g must be below long-run economic growth.** A company
compounding faster than the economy forever eventually becomes the whole economy. In
practice g sits between zero and expected long-run inflation-plus-a-little — commonly
2–3% for developed markets.

**Exit multiple.** Apply a multiple to the final year's EBITDA, as if selling then.
Grounded in observable market pricing, but it imports today's multiple into a distant
future, which quietly assumes the market will look the same.

Good practice is to compute both. If they disagree wildly, an assumption somewhere is
unreasonable.

## Why sensitivity tables exist

Because the honest answer is a range. Vary WACC across the columns and terminal growth
down the rows, and present the grid.

The grid is more informative than any single cell. If plausible inputs give ₹800 to
₹1,400 per share and the stock trades at ₹900, you have learned something real: the
market is inside your range, and the model is not going to settle the argument.

The failure mode to avoid: choosing the cell you like and presenting it as the answer.

## What to take away

1. WACC blends the cost of debt and equity by market-value weights, with debt tax-adjusted.
2. Cost of debt is observable; cost of equity is estimated, and CAPM is a convention.
3. Terminal value usually dominates, and terminal growth must stay below economic growth.
4. Present the sensitivity grid. A single number hides the uncertainty that matters.
