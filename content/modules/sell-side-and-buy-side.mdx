Two firms both employ people who stare at screens and move billions. One is called
sell-side, the other buy-side. The names sound like they describe what the firm did in
its last trade. They do not.

## The distinction is about role, not direction

The **sell-side** manufactures and distributes financial products. It creates
securities, finds buyers, makes markets in them afterwards, and publishes research on
them. Investment banks, brokerages and market makers are sell-side.

The **buy-side** owns those products. It takes client money — or its own — and decides
what to hold. Pension funds, mutual funds, insurers, hedge funds, sovereign wealth
funds and endowments are buy-side.

<Callout type="warning" title="Why this feels backwards at first">
An investment bank buys securities constantly. It is still sell-side. A pension fund
sells constantly. It is still buy-side. The label describes which side of the
manufacturing relationship the firm sits on, not the direction of any given trade.
</Callout>

## The sell-side ladder

Sell-side firms are usually sorted into three loose tiers. The boundaries are informal
and firms move between them.

**Bulge bracket.** The largest global banks. Full-service: advisory, underwriting,
sales and trading, research, and a balance sheet big enough to lend into a deal. If a
company needs $8bn of committed financing on signing, this is who can write it.

**Middle market and regional.** Serve companies too small to interest a bulge bracket.
Often deeper in a specific country or sector than the global firms.

**Boutiques.** Two flavours, and the difference matters. *Independent advisory* firms
do M&A advice and nothing else — no trading desk, no underwriting, no lending. Their
pitch is precisely that absence: with no securities to sell you and no balance sheet to
protect, their advice has fewer conflicts attached. *Sector boutiques* specialise
narrowly instead — healthcare, energy, technology — and compete on knowing an industry
better than a generalist can.

## The buy-side, briefly

The main axis is what the money is for and how patient it is.

- **Pension funds and insurers** have liabilities decades out. Long horizon, heavily
  regulated, mostly conservative.
- **Mutual funds** manage retail money against a benchmark, with daily redemptions.
- **Hedge funds** take concentrated positions, often with leverage, and charge
  performance fees.
- **Private equity** buys whole companies rather than shares of them, and holds for
  years rather than seconds.

## Where the money comes from

This is the part that explains behaviour better than any org chart.

Sell-side revenue is **transactional**: advisory fees on completion, underwriting
spreads, trading spreads. It is lumpy, and in a quiet quarter it is close to zero.
Buy-side revenue is **a percentage of assets**, charged annually whether markets rise
or fall, sometimes with a performance fee on top.

That difference explains a great deal. A sell-side banker whose bonus depends on deals
closing has a structural interest in deals closing. This is not a character flaw; it is
an incentive, and the useful response is to know it is there when you read a pitch.

## League tables

Banks are ranked publicly by deal volume and fee income — by value, by count, by region,
by sector. These rankings are called **league tables**, and they matter more than an
outsider would guess, because a bank's position is used in the next pitch.

They are also gameable. A bank can slice the table until it leads one: "number one in
Indian mid-market healthcare M&A by deal count." Volume is not skill, and a firm that
advised on the most deals is not necessarily the firm whose clients did best. Read the
qualifiers.

## What to take away

1. Sell-side manufactures and distributes; buy-side owns. The label is about role in
   the system, not the direction of a trade.
2. Bulge bracket brings a balance sheet; independent boutiques sell the absence of one.
3. Fee structure predicts behaviour more reliably than any stated mission.
4. Every league table ranking has qualifiers. They are where the ranking was won.
