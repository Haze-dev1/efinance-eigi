Before anything else in a buyout means anything, one table has to balance. It is called
sources and uses, and it states where every rupee comes from and where every rupee goes.

## The table

**Uses** — what the money is needed for:

- Purchase of the target's equity
- Repayment of the target's existing debt, which usually becomes repayable on a change
  of control
- Transaction fees: advisory, financing, legal, accounting
- Cash left on the balance sheet so the business can operate from day one

**Sources** — where it comes from:

- Senior secured debt (term loans, revolving facility)
- Subordinated or high-yield debt, and mezzanine
- Sponsor equity
- Management rollover, where the existing team reinvests rather than cashing out
- Cash already on the target's balance sheet

Sources must equal uses exactly. The sponsor equity line is usually the **plug** —
whatever is left after the debt markets say how much they will lend.

## A worked example

Target with ₹125 crore EBITDA, bought at 8.0x.

**Uses**
| | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Enterprise value at 8.0x EBITDA | 1,000 |
| Repay existing debt | 150 |
| Fees | 40 |
| Cash to balance sheet | 20 |
| **Total uses** | **1,210** |

**Sources**
| | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Senior secured term loan, 3.5x EBITDA | 437 |
| High-yield notes, 1.5x EBITDA | 188 |
| Target's existing cash | 60 |
| Management rollover | 25 |
| **Sponsor equity (plug)** | **500** |
| **Total sources** | **1,210** |

Total leverage is 5.0x EBITDA. Sponsor equity is about 41% of the total — reasonable
for a stable business, high for an aggressive market, low for a cyclical one.

<Callout type="note" title="Why fees deserve a second look">
₹40 crore of fees on a ₹1,000 crore deal is 4%, and that is not unusual once advisory,
financing, legal and accounting are added together. They are paid at closing, from the
same pot as the purchase price, which means every rupee of fees is a rupee of sponsor
equity that bought nothing.

This is also why financing fees are amortised rather than expensed at once — and why an
analyst reading a model should check whether fees were included in uses at all. Omitting
them is a common and flattering error.
</Callout>

## What limits the debt

Not the sponsor's appetite. Lenders set the ceiling, using a few tests:

- **Total leverage**, as a multiple of EBITDA. What lenders will accept moves with the
  credit cycle — the same business supports meaningfully more debt in a loose market than
  a tight one.
- **Interest coverage**: EBITDA divided by cash interest. Lenders want comfortable
  headroom, not a business that just barely services its debt.
- **Loan-to-value**, particularly where the debt is secured on specific assets.

Because the ceiling moves with the cycle, the same company purchased in two different
years can carry entirely different capital structures — which is one reason buyout
vintages perform so differently.

## The layers of debt

Senior secured debt sits at the front: first claim on assets, lowest interest rate,
strictest covenants, amortising. High-yield notes rank behind it: higher rate, looser
terms, usually repayable in one bullet at maturity. Mezzanine sits behind both and often
carries warrants or payment-in-kind interest, where interest accrues into the principal
rather than being paid in cash.

Each layer is priced for its position in the waterfall — the same logic as ordinary debt
and equity, applied at finer resolution.

## Management rollover

Worth its own note. Sponsors usually require the management team to reinvest a
meaningful part of their proceeds into the new structure. The purpose is alignment: a
chief executive who has taken all their money off the table and one who has most of
their net worth in the equity behave differently over the next five years.

## What to take away

1. Sources must equal uses; sponsor equity is the plug.
2. Uses include refinancing existing debt and fees, not just the purchase price.
3. Lenders set the leverage ceiling, and that ceiling moves with the credit cycle.
4. Management rollover exists to align incentives, not to raise money.
