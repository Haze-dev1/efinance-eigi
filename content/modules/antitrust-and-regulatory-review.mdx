A signed deal can still fail. The most common reason a large transaction dies after
announcement is not financing or diligence — it is a regulator deciding the combination
harms competition.

## What competition regulators are for

Their mandate is consumers, not shareholders. The question is not whether the merger is
good for the merging parties; it is whether the combined firm could raise prices, reduce
quality, or slow innovation because customers have nowhere else to go.

In India this is the **Competition Commission of India**. In the United States, the
**Department of Justice Antitrust Division** and the **Federal Trade Commission** share
the work. The European Commission covers the EU. A genuinely global deal may need
clearance from a dozen agencies, and any one of them can block it.

## Notification thresholds

Above certain size thresholds, parties must notify the regulator and wait before
closing. In the US this is the **Hart-Scott-Rodino** premerger notification programme:
file, then observe a waiting period during which the agencies review. India operates a
similar mandatory combination notification regime.

Closing without clearing this process — **gun jumping** — is itself an offence,
separate from whatever the regulator concludes about the merger.

## The three shapes of merger

**Horizontal.** Direct competitors. This is where scrutiny concentrates, because
removing a competitor is the most direct way to reduce competition.

**Vertical.** Buyer and supplier. The concern is foreclosure: the combined firm might
refuse to supply, or supply on worse terms to, its own downstream rivals.

**Conglomerate.** Unrelated businesses. Usually cleared quickly.

## How competitive harm is assessed

**Market definition first.** This is the most contested step, because it largely
determines the answer. Define the market narrowly — "premium noise-cancelling
headphones" — and shares look concentrated. Define it broadly — "consumer audio" — and
they do not. Both sides spend heavily on economists arguing about it.

**Then concentration.** Agencies measure how concentrated the market is before and after,
commonly using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, which sums the squares of each firm's
market share. Squaring is what makes it responsive to large players rather than treating
all firms alike.

**Then everything else.** Barriers to entry, buyer power, whether the target was a
particularly aggressive competitor, and whether efficiencies would be passed to
customers.

## The possible outcomes

- **Unconditional clearance.**
- **Conditional clearance with remedies.** Structural remedies mean divesting a business
  or brand; behavioural remedies mean commitments about future conduct. Agencies
  generally prefer structural remedies, because they do not require years of monitoring
  to know whether they worked.
- **Prohibition**, or an outcome that amounts to it: the parties abandon the deal rather
  than litigate.

<Callout type="warning" title="Why the reverse break fee is the number everyone argues about">
Regulatory risk lands on a specific question: if the deal is blocked, who has lost?

The target has been off the market for a year, has told its staff and customers it is
being acquired, and has run a business under a buyer's restrictions. That is real damage.

So targets demand a **reverse break fee** — a payment from the buyer if the deal fails on
antitrust grounds. On deals with genuine overlap it is frequently the most heavily
negotiated term in the agreement, and it is the clearest signal of how much regulatory
risk each side privately thinks exists.
</Callout>

## What to take away

1. Regulators protect competition and consumers, not the merging parties.
2. Notification is mandatory above thresholds, and closing early is its own offence.
3. Market definition is the most contested step because it largely decides the outcome.
4. The reverse break fee prices regulatory risk, and how hard it is fought tells you how
   real that risk is.
