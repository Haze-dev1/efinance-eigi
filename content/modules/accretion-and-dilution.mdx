When a board is shown an acquisition, the first question is almost always the same: what
does it do to earnings per share? The answer is called accretion or dilution, it is
easy to compute, and it is a much weaker test of value than the attention it receives
suggests.

## The mechanics

Earnings per share is net income divided by share count. An acquisition changes both.

**Numerator.** Add the target's net income. Add after-tax synergies. Subtract the
after-tax interest on any new debt. If shares were issued, no interest cost arises.

If cash on hand was used, also subtract the interest income that cash was earning.

**Denominator.** Add any new shares issued to pay for the deal. If paid entirely in
cash, the denominator does not move.

If pro-forma EPS is higher than the acquirer's standalone EPS, the deal is
**accretive**. If lower, **dilutive**.

## A worked example

Acquirer: net income ₹100 crore, 100 crore shares, so EPS ₹1.00. It buys a target
earning ₹20 crore for ₹200 crore.

**Paid in cash, borrowed at 8%, tax 25%.**
New interest = ₹16 crore, after tax ₹12 crore.
Net income = 100 + 20 − 12 = ₹108 crore. Shares unchanged at 100 crore.
EPS = ₹1.08. **Accretive.**

**Paid in shares, acquirer trading at ₹20 per share.**
₹200 crore ÷ ₹20 = 10 crore new shares.
Net income = ₹120 crore. Shares = 110 crore.
EPS = ₹1.09. **Accretive.**

## The rule that explains most outcomes

Compare the cost of the funding to the earnings yield of what you are buying.

The target's earnings yield here is 20/200 = 10%. Debt costs 8% before tax, 6% after.
10% exceeds 6%, so the deal adds to EPS.

For a share-funded deal, compare P/E ratios. The acquirer trades at 20x; the target is
being bought at 200/20 = 10x. **A company with a higher P/E buying one with a lower P/E
is accretive, arithmetically, before any synergies at all.**

<Callout type="warning" title="Why accretion is a weak test of value">
That last sentence is the problem. A high-multiple company can make almost any
low-multiple acquisition look accretive without creating a rupee of value — it is a
consequence of the arithmetic, not evidence about the business.

Worse, the reverse is also true: a genuinely excellent acquisition of a fast-growing,
high-multiple company will usually be dilutive in year one, because you are paying now
for earnings that arrive later.

Accretion measures the near-term effect of a financing choice on a per-share number. It
does not measure whether the price was worth paying.
</Callout>

## What it is legitimately good for

Two things.

It is a **communication constraint**. Public-company boards care because the market
reacts to reported EPS, and a dilutive deal is harder to explain. That is a real
consequence even if the underlying logic is weak.

It is a **quick sanity check**. A deal that is heavily dilutive for years demands an
explanation. Sometimes that explanation is excellent. But it needs to exist.

## The right questions to ask alongside it

- What is the internal rate of return on the total consideration, including synergies?
- Does the return exceed the acquirer's cost of capital?
- How confident are the synergies, and are they cost or revenue?
- What does the deal do to leverage, and what happens in a downturn?

## What to take away

1. Accretion compares the earnings yield of what you buy to the after-tax cost of
   funding it.
2. Higher-P/E buying lower-P/E is accretive by arithmetic, with no value created.
3. Good acquisitions of growing companies are often dilutive early.
4. Use it as a communication constraint and a sanity check, never as the value test.
