Ask ten people what an investment bank does and you will get ten answers, most of
them describing one desk on one floor. The confusion is reasonable: the phrase covers
several businesses that happen to share a building, a balance sheet, and a brand.

## The core job: moving capital between people who have it and people who need it

A company needs money to build a factory. A pension fund has money and needs it to
grow. Neither can easily find the other, agree a price, or trust the paperwork. That
gap is the business. An investment bank stands in the middle and takes a fee for
closing it.

Everything else is a variation on that theme.

## The divisions, and why they are separated

**Investment Banking Division (IBD).** Advises companies. Two halves: *coverage*
bankers who own the relationship with a client or sector, and *product* teams who
execute the deal — mergers and acquisitions, equity capital markets, debt capital
markets. When a company sells itself, buys a rival, or lists on an exchange, IBD runs
it and charges a percentage of the transaction.

**Sales and Trading.** Buys and sells securities — sometimes for clients, sometimes
with the bank's own money. Makes money on spread and volume rather than on advice.

**Research.** Publishes analysis and price targets on companies. Its output is read
by the same investors that Sales talks to.

**Asset and Wealth Management.** Manages money on behalf of clients for a fee on
assets under management.

Here is the part that matters, and that most explanations skip: **these divisions are
legally required to be separated.** The barrier is called the Chinese wall, or more
plainly an information barrier.

The reason is a conflict of interest that is easy to state. IBD knows, weeks in
advance, that Company A is about to buy Company B. That information is worth a
fortune to a trader. Letting it cross the floor is not sharp practice — it is
insider dealing, and it is criminal. So IBD sits on what the industry calls the
**private side**: it holds material non-public information and cannot trade. Sales,
Trading and Research sit on the **public side**: they trade freely, on public
information only.

<Callout type="warning" title="The single most useful distinction to learn early">
Private side means you know things the market does not, and therefore cannot trade.
Public side means you can trade, and therefore are not told. Almost every rule about
who may speak to whom inside a bank follows from this one line.
</Callout>

## Sell-side and buy-side

A second split, and a different one — this one is between firms, not within them.

The **sell-side** creates and sells securities: investment banks, brokerages, market
makers. The **buy-side** buys and holds them: pension funds, mutual funds, insurers,
hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds.

The names are from the sell-side's point of view, which is why they feel backwards
the first time. An investment bank is sell-side even when it is buying, because its
role in the system is to manufacture and distribute the product.

## How the fees actually work

Worth internalising, because it explains a lot of behaviour:

- **M&A advisory** — a success fee, a percentage of deal value, paid mostly on
  completion. A deal that collapses at the last minute pays very little.
- **Underwriting** — the bank buys the issue from the company and resells it. The
  gross spread is the difference.
- **Trading** — bid-ask spread and volume.
- **Asset management** — a percentage of assets under management, charged annually
  whether or not the fund performs.

Notice that two of those are paid only if something happens, and two are paid
regardless. That difference shapes the culture of each desk more than any org chart.

## What to take away

1. "Investment bank" is a holding company for several distinct businesses.
2. The wall between them exists because the alternative is a crime, not because of
   office politics.
3. Sell-side manufactures and distributes; buy-side owns.
4. Fee structure predicts behaviour. Follow the fee.
