Here is a question that separates people who have read about markets from people who
understand them:

*When you buy shares of Infosys on the NSE, how much of your money reaches Infosys?*

None of it. Not a rupee.

If that is surprising, this module is the most valuable ten minutes you will spend
here, because almost everything else in capital markets sits on top of this
distinction.

## Two different markets wearing the same word

The **primary market** is where a security is created and sold for the first time.
The company issues new shares, investors pay for them, and **the money goes to the
company**. This happens once per issue.

The **secondary market** is where those securities are traded afterwards, between
investors. Someone sells, someone buys, money moves between them. **The company is
not a party to the transaction** and receives nothing. This happens continuously,
for as long as the security exists.

Your NSE trade is secondary. The person on the other side got a slightly better or
worse price than they hoped. Infosys, sitting in Bengaluru, is unaffected.

## So why does any company care about its share price?

If secondary trading sends the company no money, why do management teams watch the
price so closely?

Four reasons, and they are worth knowing because "the price is just sentiment" is a
common and wrong conclusion to draw here:

1. **The next raise is priced off it.** A company issuing new shares later sells them
   at a discount to the prevailing market price. A higher price means fewer shares
   sold for the same money — less dilution for existing owners.
2. **Acquisition currency.** Companies buy other companies with stock. Expensive
   stock buys more.
3. **Employee compensation.** Options and RSUs are a real part of pay. A falling price
   is a pay cut that shows up in resignations.
4. **Takeover defence.** A depressed price makes the company cheap to buy.

The secondary market does not fund the company. It sets the terms on which the
company can do almost everything else.

## What happens in a primary issue

The sequence, compressed:

1. **Appointment.** The company hires investment banks — the lead manager runs the
   process.
2. **Due diligence and drafting.** The offer document is prepared. In India this is
   filed with SEBI; in the US, a registration statement goes to the SEC.
3. **Regulatory review.** The regulator comments. The document is revised. This is
   disclosure review, not merit review — the regulator is checking that investors are
   told enough, not that the investment is good.
4. **Marketing.** Roadshows, meetings, and a book of investor demand at various
   prices.
5. **Pricing and allotment.** A final price is set, shares are allotted.
6. **Listing.** The shares begin trading — and from that first trade onward,
   everything is secondary.

<Callout type="note" title="A regulator is not a quality filter">
SEBI and the SEC both review disclosure, not merit. Approval means the risks were
disclosed adequately. It is not an opinion that the shares are worth buying, and
plenty of duly-approved IPOs have gone on to lose investors money.
</Callout>

## Kinds of primary issue

- **IPO** — the first sale of shares to the public.
- **Follow-on public offer (FPO)** — a further public sale by an already-listed
  company.
- **Rights issue** — new shares offered to existing shareholders in proportion to
  what they already hold, usually at a discount.
- **Private placement / preferential allotment** — shares sold to a selected group of
  investors rather than the public.
- **Offer for sale (OFS)** — and this one is the trap. Existing shareholders sell
  *their* shares to the public. New shares are not created; the company receives
  nothing. An OFS is printed in the same prospectus as an IPO and is economically a
  secondary transaction wearing primary clothes.

Many real IPOs are a mix: a fresh issue component that funds the company, plus an
offer-for-sale component that pays out early investors. **Read the split.** It tells
you whether you are funding a business or buying out its founders.

## What to take away

1. Primary creates securities and funds the company. Secondary transfers them between
   investors and does not.
2. Secondary prices still govern dilution, acquisitions, pay, and takeover risk.
3. Regulatory approval is a disclosure check, never an endorsement.
4. In any IPO, find the fresh-issue versus offer-for-sale split before anything else.
