A multiple is a valuation compressed into one number. That compression is what makes it
useful and what makes it dangerous.

## What a multiple is

A ratio of value to a financial measure. EV/EBITDA of 12x says the business is valued at
twelve times its annual operating earnings.

The compression is the point: it lets you compare businesses of different sizes in one
glance. The cost is that everything that produced the number — growth rate, margin,
capital intensity, risk — is now invisible.

## Matching numerator to denominator

This is the rule that most errors violate, and it has a simple logic: **the numerator
and denominator must refer to the same claimants.**

Enterprise value belongs to everyone who funded the business — lenders and
shareholders. So it must be paired with a profit measure calculated *before* lenders are
paid:

- EV / EBITDA
- EV / EBIT
- EV / Revenue

Equity value belongs to shareholders alone, so it pairs with profit measured *after*
interest:

- Price / Earnings
- Price / Book

<Callout type="warning" title="The error that gives nonsense">
EV / Net income mixes the two. Net income is after interest, so it is what shareholders
get; enterprise value includes the lenders' claim. The ratio compares a whole-business
value against a shareholders-only profit, and its answer means nothing. Same for
P/EBITDA.
</Callout>

## Why bankers prefer EV/EBITDA

Two identical businesses, one heavily borrowed, will have very different P/E ratios
purely because interest reduces the borrower's net income. EV/EBITDA neutralises that:
it compares the operating business before financing choices are made.

EBITDA also adds back depreciation and amortisation, which makes companies with
different asset ages and accounting policies more comparable.

## What EBITDA hides

EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Adding
depreciation back is defensible for comparison. Treating it as a proxy for cash flow is
not.

Depreciation is a rough stand-in for the fact that assets wear out and must be replaced.
A steel plant genuinely must spend to keep running; ignoring that does not make the
spending optional. For a capital-intensive business, EBITDA overstates what is actually
available to investors, sometimes enormously. For an asset-light software business the
gap is small.

The useful discipline: whenever someone quotes EBITDA for a heavy-asset business, ask
what capital expenditure runs at.

## Reading a multiple properly

A multiple is only meaningful against something. 12x is high or low only relative to:

- **Peers.** What do comparable businesses trade at today?
- **History.** What has this company traded at over five years?
- **Fundamentals.** Higher growth, higher margins and lower risk all justify a higher
  multiple. If a company trades above its peers, the question is whether its
  fundamentals explain the gap.

A company on 20x when its peers are on 12x is not automatically expensive. It is
expensive *unless* it grows faster, earns better margins, or is less risky. Those are
checkable claims.

## What to take away

1. A multiple compresses a valuation; what it compresses is what you must go and check.
2. Numerator and denominator must cover the same claimants. EV with pre-interest
   profit, equity value with post-interest profit.
3. EV/EBITDA compares operations independent of financing.
4. EBITDA is not cash flow. For asset-heavy businesses the difference is the whole
   argument.
