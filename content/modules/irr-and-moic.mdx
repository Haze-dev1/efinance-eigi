Two numbers describe the outcome of a buyout, and they routinely disagree. Knowing which
question each answers stops you being misled by whichever one a sponsor chose to put on
the slide.

## MOIC

**Multiple on invested capital.** Total cash returned divided by total cash invested.

Invest ₹300 crore, receive ₹1,100 crore, MOIC is 3.7x.

It is blunt and honest: how many times did I get my money back? It says nothing at all
about how long that took.

## IRR

**Internal rate of return.** The annualised rate at which the investment compounded. It
is the discount rate that makes the present value of all cash flows equal zero — there
is no closed-form solution, so it is solved numerically.

For a single investment and a single exit, the relationship is simple:

    IRR = MOIC^(1/years) − 1

3.7x over five years is 29.9% a year. The same 3.7x over eight years is 17.8%.

## Why they disagree

Consider two deals:

| | Deal A | Deal B |
|---|---|---|
| Invested | ₹300 crore | ₹300 crore |
| Returned | ₹600 crore | ₹900 crore |
| Years held | 2 | 7 |
| MOIC | 2.0x | 3.0x |
| IRR | 41.4% | 17.0% |

Deal B returned half as much again in absolute terms. Deal A compounded at more than
twice the rate. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

**MOIC asks: how much money did I make?**
**IRR asks: how hard did my money work per year?**

<Callout type="warning" title="How IRR gets flattered">
IRR is sensitive to timing, and that sensitivity can be engineered without improving the
business.

**Early distributions.** A dividend recapitalisation — borrowing more against the
company and paying the proceeds to the sponsor — returns cash sooner. IRR rises sharply.
The company is now carrying more debt and is more fragile, which IRR does not show.

**Subscription lines.** A fund borrows short-term to pay for an investment and calls
capital from its investors months later. The clock on IRR starts when investors' money
is actually drawn, so delaying the call raises the reported IRR without changing a thing
about the underlying deal.

**Fast flips.** A quick sale at a modest multiple can post a spectacular IRR. It also
returns little money, and the investor now has to find somewhere else to put it.

None of this is fraud, and all of it is why serious investors read MOIC and IRR
together, alongside the holding period.
</Callout>

## What the sponsor is aiming for

Buyout funds have historically targeted something like 20–25% gross IRR and 2.0–3.0x
MOIC over a three-to-five-year hold. Those are targets, not outcomes, and the dispersion
between funds is wide.

Note also that gross returns are not what investors receive. Management fees, typically
charged on committed capital, and carried interest, typically around 20% of gains above
a hurdle, sit between gross and net. The gap is substantial and it is why the
distinction matters.

## Working backwards

In practice sponsors use the arithmetic in reverse. Fix the required return, fix a
realistic exit multiple, and solve for the entry price you can afford. The model does
not tell you what the company is worth; it tells you the most you can pay and still hit
your target.

That is a genuinely different question from the one a DCF answers, and it explains why a
sponsor and a strategic buyer bidding for the same asset can arrive at very different
numbers without either being irrational.

## What to take away

1. MOIC measures how much; IRR measures how fast. Read them together.
2. The bridge for a single investment is IRR = MOIC^(1/years) − 1.
3. IRR can be raised by timing alone — dividend recaps, subscription lines, fast flips.
4. Sponsors solve backwards from a required return to a maximum entry price.
