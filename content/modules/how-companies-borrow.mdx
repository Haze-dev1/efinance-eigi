"The company raised debt" covers instruments that behave very differently. The
differences matter most at the two moments that count: when terms are negotiated, and
when something goes wrong.

## Loans and bonds

A **loan** is a private agreement with one bank or a small group of them. Terms are
negotiated bilaterally, the lender knows the borrower, and if the borrower gets into
difficulty there is a short list of people to call.

A **bond** is a security sold to many investors and traded afterwards. Terms are
standardised because they must be identical for every holder. The borrower may have no
idea who holds its bonds at any moment.

That difference drives most of the rest. Loans are flexible and renegotiable. Bonds are
cheaper at scale and can be issued in larger size, but restructuring one means dealing
with a dispersed and often anonymous group.

## The main forms

**Term loan.** A fixed amount, drawn once, repaid on a schedule. Usually secured,
usually floating rate.

**Revolving credit facility.** A limit the company can draw, repay and redraw, like an
overdraft. Used for working capital swings. The company pays a commitment fee on the
undrawn portion for the privilege of having it available — which is the point: it is
insurance against a bad month.

**Bonds.** Fixed coupon, repaid in a single bullet at maturity. Investment grade bonds
carry light covenants; high-yield bonds carry more.

**Commercial paper.** Very short-term unsecured borrowing, days to months, available
only to strong credits. Cheap — and it must be rolled over constantly, which is exactly
the exposure that turns a liquidity scare into a crisis.

## Secured and unsecured

**Secured** debt has specific assets pledged. On default the lender can take those
assets. Lower rate, tighter restrictions on what the company may do with the collateral.

**Unsecured** debt ranks behind secured claims and relies on the borrower's general
creditworthiness. Higher rate.

The distinction only bites in a default, which is why it is under-appreciated in good
times and decisive in bad ones.

<Callout type="note" title="Reading a debt quote">
Corporate debt is usually quoted as a **spread over a benchmark** — say "SOFR plus 350
basis points" or "G-sec plus 250". A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point,
so 350bp is 3.5%.

Two things follow. The spread is the compensation for *this borrower's* credit risk,
separate from the general level of rates. And with a floating benchmark, the borrower's
interest cost moves with the market even though the spread is fixed at issue.
</Callout>

## What lenders require

Beyond the rate, a loan agreement constrains behaviour:

- **Financial covenants** — leverage and coverage ratios to be maintained
- **Negative covenants** — limits on further borrowing, asset sales, dividends
- **Security and guarantees** — what is pledged, and which group companies stand behind
  the debt
- **Reporting** — regular financial information

The pattern is consistent: the weaker the credit, the more the lender substitutes
control for rate.

## What happens in a default

Not immediate seizure. Usually:

1. **Waiver or amendment.** The lender agrees to overlook the breach, for a fee and
   usually tighter terms.
2. **Restructuring.** Maturities extended, rates adjusted, sometimes debt exchanged for
   equity.
3. **Enforcement.** Security is called, or insolvency proceedings begin.

Lenders generally prefer a working borrower to a liquidated one — a going concern is
worth more than its parts — which is why steps one and two are far more common than step
three.

## What to take away

1. Loans are private and renegotiable; bonds are standardised, cheaper at scale, harder
   to restructure.
2. A revolver is insurance, and the commitment fee is its premium.
3. Secured versus unsecured is irrelevant until default, then decisive.
4. Weak credits pay in control as well as in interest.
