When a company issues securities, someone has to bear the risk that they do not all
sell. Who bears it, and what they are paid for bearing it, is the substance of
underwriting.

## Two arrangements

**Firm commitment underwriting.** The bank buys the entire issue from the company at an
agreed price and resells it. The company's proceeds are certain the moment the deal is
priced. If demand collapses, the bank owns the unsold stock and takes the loss.

**Best efforts.** The bank agrees to try. Unsold securities are simply not sold, and the
company raises less than it hoped. The risk stays with the issuer.

The difference is the whole point: in a firm commitment the bank has taken a real
position, and it is paid accordingly.

## The gross spread

The bank's compensation is the difference between what it pays the issuer and what it
sells at. Sell at ₹100 and pay the issuer ₹95, and the gross spread is ₹5 — 5%.

It splits three ways:

- **Management fee** — to the banks that structured and ran the deal
- **Underwriting fee** — compensation for bearing the risk of unsold stock
- **Selling concession** — the largest share, to whoever actually placed the shares with
  investors

The concession being largest is the tell: distribution is what is scarce. Anyone can
draft a prospectus; reaching the investors who will actually buy at scale is the part
that is hard to replicate.

## Syndication

Large issues are not underwritten alone. A **syndicate** spreads the risk across banks,
and spreads the distribution across their client bases too.

- **Lead manager / bookrunner** runs the process, builds the book, takes the largest
  economics and the largest reputational exposure.
- **Co-managers** take smaller allocations and contribute distribution.
- Loan syndications add further tiers of participants taking smaller pieces.

Two reasons to syndicate, and both are real: no single bank wants a billion-dollar
position, and no single bank reaches every investor.

<Callout type="note" title="What the fee actually pays for">
It is tempting to read a gross spread as expensive. It is buying three things:

**Risk transfer.** In a firm commitment the issuer's proceeds are certain regardless of
what the market does between pricing and settlement.

**Distribution.** Access to institutional investors that a company cannot reach alone.

**Price discovery and certification.** The book-building process finds the clearing
price, and a reputable bank attaching its name to an issue is itself a signal — the bank
is risking a reputation it needs for the next hundred deals.

Whether it is worth it is a fair question, and it is one the rise of direct listings is
implicitly testing.
</Callout>

## Typical levels

Fee levels vary widely by market, size and type. The consistent pattern is that spreads
are much larger for equity than for investment-grade debt, and larger for small issues
than for large ones. Equity is harder to price and harder to place; a large
investment-grade bond from a well-known issuer largely sells itself.

For a specific deal, the actual figure is disclosed. The underwriting section of an offer
document sets out the spread and the syndicate, and reading it is more reliable than any
rule of thumb.

## Conflicts worth naming

The bank advises the issuer on price while also selling to investors it deals with
repeatedly. A lower price makes the issue easier to place and pleases those investors.
The bank's own fee falls only slightly, because it is a percentage of a somewhat smaller
number.

This does not make underwriters dishonest. It means the interests are not perfectly
aligned, and an issuer who understands where the tension sits negotiates better.

## What to take away

1. Firm commitment transfers placement risk to the bank; best efforts leaves it with the
   issuer.
2. The gross spread splits into management, underwriting and selling components, and the
   selling concession is largest.
3. Syndication spreads both risk and distribution reach.
4. The fee buys risk transfer, distribution and certification — and the adviser's
   interests are close to, but not identical with, the issuer's.
