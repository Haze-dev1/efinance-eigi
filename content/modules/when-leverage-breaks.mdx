Most buyouts work. The ones that fail follow a recognisable pattern, and the pattern is
always the same underlying fact: a fixed obligation met with a variable cash flow.

## The mechanism

Interest is due on schedule. Revenue is not. In a good year the gap is comfortable. In a
bad year the interest bill does not adjust, and a business that would have survived
unleveraged has no room.

This is not a subtle failure mode. It is the entire risk, restated.

## Covenants

Lenders do not wait for a missed payment. Loan agreements contain **covenants** —
conditions the borrower must keep meeting — designed to trigger long before the money
actually runs out.

**Maintenance covenants** are tested every quarter regardless of what the company does:
leverage below an agreed multiple of EBITDA, interest coverage above an agreed level.

**Incurrence covenants** are tested only when the company takes an action: raising more
debt, paying a dividend, making an acquisition.

Breaching a covenant is a **default** even if every payment has been made on time. What
follows is usually a negotiation rather than an immediate seizure, but the leverage has
moved: the sponsor now needs something from the lenders, and lenders charge for what
they are asked to provide — a higher rate, tighter terms, fees, sometimes equity.

<Callout type="warning" title="Cov-lite, and why the failure mode changed">
Through the 2010s, competition among lenders produced **covenant-lite** loans with
incurrence covenants only and no quarterly maintenance test.

The consequence is not that companies stopped failing. It is that the early-warning
system was removed. Without a maintenance test, deterioration is not flagged quarterly;
problems surface later and larger, when a payment is actually missed or a maturity
arrives that cannot be refinanced. Less frequent defaults, worse ones when they come.
</Callout>

## How the failure actually unfolds

1. **Demand falls**, or input costs rise, or a large customer leaves. EBITDA drops.
2. **Leverage rises mechanically.** Debt has not changed but EBITDA has fallen, so the
   ratio worsens without any new borrowing.
3. **A covenant is breached**, or would be at the next test.
4. **Cash is diverted to debt service.** Capital expenditure and marketing are cut —
   which protects the covenant and damages the business, worsening the underlying
   problem.
5. **Refinancing is needed** and is now expensive, because the company is refinancing
   from a position of visible weakness. If credit markets have also tightened, it may
   not be available at all.
6. **Restructuring.** The usual outcome is a debt-for-equity swap: lenders write off
   part of what they are owed in exchange for ownership. The sponsor's equity is wiped
   out or heavily diluted.

## Why interest rate moves matter so much

A large share of leveraged loans carry **floating rates** — a benchmark plus a spread.
When benchmark rates rise, the interest bill rises with them, immediately, on a
structure sized when rates were lower.

A company levered at 6x EBITDA when the benchmark was near zero faces a materially
different obligation after a few points of rate increases. Nothing about its operations
changed. The financing did, and that is enough.

## What actually protects a structure

- **Genuine headroom** rather than the maximum the market would lend
- **Fixed-rate or hedged debt**, so the cost does not move with the cycle
- **Long maturities**, so no refinancing is forced during a bad patch
- **A business whose revenue does not collapse in a downturn**

Each of these lowers the return in the good case. That is precisely the trade, and it is
made — explicitly or by default — at the moment the structure is set.

## What to take away

1. A fixed obligation against variable cash flow is the whole of the risk.
2. Covenant breach is default without any missed payment, and it shifts leverage to
   lenders.
3. Cov-lite made defaults less frequent and later, not less severe.
4. Floating-rate debt means rate moves alone can break a structure that was operating fine.
