A profitable company can run out of money and fail. If that sounds like a contradiction,
the three financial statements are where the contradiction is resolved.

## What each statement answers

**Income statement (profit and loss).** Did the business make a profit over a period?
Revenue minus costs, over a quarter or a year.

**Balance sheet.** What does it own and owe, at one instant? A photograph, not a film.
Assets = liabilities + equity, always, by construction.

**Cash flow statement.** Where did cash actually move over the period, and why?

Three views of the same business: performance over time, position at a moment, and
liquidity over time.

## Why profit is not cash

Accounting recognises revenue when it is *earned*, not when the money arrives. Sell
₹10 crore of goods on 90-day credit and you book ₹10 crore of revenue today and receive
nothing for three months. Meanwhile you have paid your suppliers and your staff.

This gap is **working capital**, and it is where fast-growing companies die. Growth
consumes cash before it produces it: you buy inventory and extend credit ahead of being
paid. A business growing 60% a year can be genuinely profitable and genuinely insolvent
at the same time.

<Callout type="warning" title="The one sentence worth memorising">
Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Profit depends on judgements about when to
recognise revenue, how fast to depreciate an asset, and what to provide for. Cash in the
bank is checkable.
</Callout>

## The cash flow statement, in three parts

**Operating.** Cash from running the business. Starts with net profit and reverses the
non-cash items — depreciation and amortisation added back, working capital movements
subtracted or added. Over a few years this should look like profit. If it persistently
does not, something is wrong.

**Investing.** Cash spent on or received from long-lived assets. Capital expenditure
lives here.

**Financing.** Cash from raising or repaying debt and equity, and dividends paid.

A useful reflex: a business with strong operating cash flow, modest investing outflow
and no reliance on financing inflows is funding itself. One whose operating line is
negative and whose financing line keeps it alive is running on somebody else's money.

## Depreciation, the standard confusion

Buy a machine for ₹100 crore lasting ten years. Cash goes out once, ₹100 crore, in year
one. But profit is reduced by ₹10 crore a year for ten years — the depreciation charge.

So depreciation is a real economic cost recognised over time, but it is not a cash
payment in the year it appears. That is why the cash flow statement adds it back: the
cash already left, years ago.

## How the three connect

They are not independent documents.

- Net profit from the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance
  sheet, and starts the cash flow statement.
- The cash line on the balance sheet is the closing balance of the cash flow statement.
- Depreciation reduces profit and reduces the asset's carrying value.

If a model's balance sheet does not balance, one of these links is broken. That is what
makes it a genuine check rather than a formality.

## What to take away

1. Performance over a period, position at an instant, cash over a period.
2. Profit and cash diverge because revenue is recognised when earned, not when received.
3. Working capital is where growing companies run out of money.
4. The statements interlock. A balance sheet that does not balance is a broken link,
   not a rounding problem.
